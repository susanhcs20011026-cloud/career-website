RESEARCH WRITING · AUDIENCE INSIGHTS

Vicarious Social Comparison: Parents’ Social Media Use and Their Expectations for Adolescent and Young Adult Children

A quantitative communication research project examining social media use, social comparison, and expectations among parents of adolescents and young adults.

Institution
University of California, Davis
Role
First-listed author; alphabetical author order
Field
Communication Research
01 / RESEARCH OVERVIEW

Research Overview

Research Question

How do parents’ social media use and tendency to compare relate to their expectations for their children, parenting attitudes, and anxiety?

Background

Social media can expose parents to idealized representations of children’s achievements and family life. This project examined vicarious social comparison—comparing someone close to us with others—and its relationship to parents’ expectations for adolescent and young adult children.

03 / RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

Research Methodology

The study combined a survey and an experiment with 295 parents of at least one child aged 15–25. Participants were recruited through students in introductory university classes.

Parents viewed three simulated social media posts depicting either highly successful or ordinary high school seniors, with each post shown for 30 seconds. Survey measures addressed social media use, social comparison orientation, parental expectations, authoritarian parenting, and anxiety.

The chapter reports no effect of the experimental condition. Its main findings concern relationships in the cross-sectional survey data, with experimental condition controlled in the analyses.

04 / KEY FINDINGS

Key Findings

Social Comparison and Expectations

A stronger tendency to engage in social comparison was associated with higher parental expectations across all five domains examined: academics, extracurricular activities, home life, social life, and general expectations.

Social Media Use and Parenting Perspectives

Greater social media use was associated with higher academic and social expectations, but not expectations in the other domains. It was also associated with more authoritarian parenting and greater anxiety. The relationship between social media use and social expectations was stronger among parents with a greater tendency to compare.

What the Findings Do—and Do Not—Show

The brief post-viewing experiment showed no effect of condition. The survey findings describe associations, not evidence that social media caused changes in parents’ expectations, parenting style, or anxiety. Recruitment through university students also limits how broadly the findings can be generalized.

05 / RESEARCH TO CLEAR COMMUNICATION

Making Research Accessible

Academic research requires precision, but communicating its implications to a broader audience also requires clarity. This project strengthened my ability to organize evidence, explain complex concepts, and communicate findings without overstating the results.

PLAIN-LANGUAGE RESEARCH SUMMARY

Parents who were more inclined to compare tended to hold higher expectations for their children. Those who spent more time on social media also reported higher academic and social expectations, more authoritarian parenting, and greater anxiety. These patterns do not tell us what caused what, and the brief experiment found no effect from viewing the different posts.
